CAPITAL-CITY PATRONAGE

One country. Very different ways of moving.

Public transport use varies substantially between Australian cities. Geography, population and the availability of different modes all shape how people move.

02

THE GEOGRAPHY OF THE COMMUTE

Public transport matters more to the commute in some parts of Australia than others.

The role of public transport differs substantially across Australia's states and territories. This map shows the share of commuters using public transport as their only method of travel to work.

Public transport share of one-method commuters

Darker regions indicate a greater reliance on public transport for the journey to work. Change the Census year to see how commuting patterns have shifted over time.

HOW TO READ

Colour represents the percentage of employed people who reported public transport as their single method of travel to work. The measure excludes people working from home and people using multiple travel methods.

Source: Bureau of Infrastructure and Transport Research Economics, Australian Infrastructure and Transport Statistics Yearbook 2025, Tables 2.4a–2.4h. Original Census data sourced by BITRE from the Australian Bureau of Statistics. Map boundaries: Rowan Hogan's Australian States GeoJSON .

03

LONG-DISTANCE CONNECTIONS

The long-distance connections that public transport still makes possible.

Public transport does not only shape movement within cities. Rail, coach and other non-air public transport continue to connect major Australian capitals, though some corridors are much busier than others.

Inter-capital passenger movements by city pair

Line thickness represents the number of passenger movements between major capital-city pairs using rail, coach and other non-air public transport. Change the financial year to compare pre-pandemic, pandemic and recovery patterns.

HOW TO READ

Each line connects a pair of capitals. Thicker and darker lines indicate more passenger movements. The flows are undirected, so each connection represents total movement between the two cities rather than one-way trips.

Source: Bureau of Infrastructure and Transport Research Economics, Australian Infrastructure and Transport Statistics Yearbook 2025, Table 2.2d: Inter-capital city passenger travel by city pair — Rail, Coach and Other.

THE RHYTHM OF THE WEEK

Melbourne’s transport network has a rhythm of its own.

Travel demand follows a recognisable weekly rhythm. Weekdays carry the regular commute, while weekends reveal a different pattern of movement across Victoria's public transport network.

A year of travel, one week at a time

Each cell represents average daily passenger boardings for a particular month and day of the week. Darker cells indicate busier days.

HOW TO READ

Read horizontally to compare weekdays with weekends. Read vertically to see how the same day changes through the year. Use the controls above the chart to compare different transport modes and years.

Source: Victorian Department of Transport and Planning, Monthly average patronage by day type and by mode. Weekday cells represent normal weekdays; Saturday and Sunday represent weekend travel.

WHEN ROUTINE CHANGES

School holidays reshape the weekday commute.

A normal weekday, a school-holiday weekday and a weekend create very different demand conditions. Looking beyond averages reveals how much patronage varies under each type of day — and whether that effect is the same across modes.

How predictable is demand on different kinds of days?

Each box summarises average daily patronage observations from 2023 to 2025. Compare the centre and spread of each distribution to see how travel changes between normal weekdays, school holidays and weekends.

School holiday weekday

The box shows the middle 50% of observations, the dark line marks the median and the whiskers show the typical range. Individual points beyond the whiskers represent unusually high or low observations.

HOW TO READ

Compare the three boxes within each transport mode. A box further to the right represents higher typical patronage. Wider boxes and longer whiskers indicate greater variation in demand. Each mode uses its own horizontal scale so that its distribution remains visible.

Source: Victorian Department of Transport and Planning, Monthly average patronage by day type and by mode. Visualisation uses observations from 2023–2025.

06

A CHANGING MIX

The mix of public transport changed after 2020.

COVID did more than reduce the number of passengers. It changed the balance between different forms of public transport. Looking at each mode as a share of the network reveals which parts of Victoria's system became relatively more or less important.

Victoria's public transport mix, month by month

Each coloured stream represents one transport mode. Stream thickness shows its share of total public transport patronage during that month rather than its raw number of passengers.

Look at the thickness of each coloured band rather than its vertical position. A wider band means that mode accounted for a larger share of public transport use during that month.

HOW TO READ

Follow one colour from left to right to track how that mode's share changes through time. Because every month is normalised to the same total, changes in band width represent changes in the transport mix rather than changes in the overall number of passengers.

Source: Victorian Department of Transport and Planning, Monthly public transport patronage by mode. Values are expressed as each mode's share of total monthly patronage.

THE PASSENGER EXPERIENCE

Passengers are highly satisfied — but not equally across every mode.

Patronage tells us how much public transport is used. Customer satisfaction tells us what that journey feels like. Transport for NSW surveys show consistently high satisfaction, although passenger experiences differ between modes.

Customer satisfaction across NSW public transport

The percentage of surveyed passengers who reported being partly to very satisfied with their public transport service. Change the survey wave to see how satisfaction has shifted over time.

WHY IT MATTERS

A larger network or higher patronage does not necessarily mean a better passenger experience. Ferry and metro users reported particularly high satisfaction, while train and bus satisfaction was lower in the May 2024 survey.

Source: Transport for NSW, Customer Satisfaction Index, May 2024. Satisfaction represents passengers who reported being partly to very satisfied.

12

CHANGING PASSENGER PERCEPTIONS

Customer satisfaction has not moved in the same direction across every mode.

Overall satisfaction remains high, but the pattern over time differs between trains, buses, ferries, light rail and metro. Small multiples make those individual trajectories easier to compare without combining them into one crowded chart.

How satisfaction changed across NSW transport modes

Each panel shows the percentage of surveyed passengers reporting satisfaction with one public transport mode across successive survey waves.

WHAT STANDS OUT

Ferry and metro satisfaction remained consistently high, while train and bus satisfaction declined more noticeably. Light rail remained between these groups, showing that the passenger experience evolved differently across modes.

Source: Transport for NSW, Customer Satisfaction Index. Survey waves shown from November 2020 to May 2024.

WHEN SERVICES FALL SHORT

Reliability problems extend beyond simply running late.

Passenger experience depends on more than whether a bus arrives. Services must run on time, avoid cancellations, remain visible in real-time systems and generate fewer complaints. Across Greater Sydney, performance against these targets has varied considerably.

How often did Greater Sydney bus operators meet their targets?

Each pair of bars shows the share of reported operator-month observations that met or missed the relevant performance KPI between January 2023 and May 2024.

15%
On-time target met

First-stop punctuality met its KPI in only a small minority of reported observations.

20%
Cancellation target met

Cancelled and incomplete-trip performance also frequently fell short of target.

70%
Last-stop tracking target met

Real-time tracking performed better than the punctuality and cancellation measures.

WHY IT MATTERS

Reliability is multidimensional. Even when tracking systems successfully record services, passengers may still experience delays, cancellations or other service failures. The contrast between these measures shows why operational performance cannot be reduced to a single number.

Source: Audit Office of New South Wales, Bus contracts in metropolitan Sydney, using Transport for NSW operator performance data. Period shown: January 2023 to May 2024.

11

PUTTING THE PIECES TOGETHER

Bigger networks tend to support greater public-transport reliance — but scale is not the whole story.

Australia’s largest public transport markets also tend to be places where public transport plays a greater role in the journey to work. But smaller cities do not follow a single pattern, showing that network size alone does not explain how communities use public transport.

Network scale versus commuting reliance

Each point pairs a capital city’s total public transport patronage in 2018–19 with the share of one-method commuters using public transport in its state or territory in the 2016 Census.

Points further right represent larger public transport networks. Points higher on the chart represent greater public-transport reliance for commuting. The dashed line summarises the overall relationship between the two measures.

THE BIG PICTURE

Sydney and Melbourne combine high patronage with relatively strong commuting reliance, while smaller capitals show more varied relationships. Public transport use is shaped by scale, but also by local travel patterns, urban form and the role each network plays in everyday life.

Source: Bureau of Infrastructure and Transport Research Economics, Australian Infrastructure and Transport Statistics Yearbook 2025. Patronage uses 2018–19 capital-city figures; commute share uses 2016 Census method-of-travel-to-work figures.
